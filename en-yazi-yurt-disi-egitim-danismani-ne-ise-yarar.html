What does a study abroad consultant do?

Working with a consultant is not required

First, a plain fact: you do not have to work with a consultant to apply to a university abroad. The University of Liverpool writes that a student can apply directly if they want. The University of Canberra also states that most universities in Australia allow self-application.

It is similar on the visa side. IRCC, the Canadian immigration agency, says that hiring a representative is not required to apply and that all applications are assessed by the same criteria.

A consultant's contribution is not opening a door, but directing the family's time and attention to the right tasks. Below we explain what this looks like in practice and where the limits are.

Collecting different countries' calendars in one place

Every country, even every university, has a different deadline. A few examples for 2027 entry:

  • United Kingdom: Applications for Oxford, Cambridge and medicine closed on 15 October 2026. For most other programs, UCAS's equal consideration deadline is 13 January 2027, at 18:00 (UK time).
  • US: Common App opens for the new cycle on 1 August every year. The Early Decision deadline at Columbia, Johns Hopkins and the University of Pennsylvania is 1 November 2026.
  • Netherlands: For programs with a quota (numerus fixus), such as medicine, the Studielink deadline is 15 January 2027. For bachelor's programs without a quota, the national deadline is 1 May.

When a student applies to two or three countries in the same year, these dates overlap. The consultant's first job is to make a single calendar for the student's list. Exam, reference and document dates are ordered around this calendar.

Building the school list around the student's profile

The list makes sense when grades, exam results, language level, interests and budget are considered together. With Common App you can apply to at most 20 schools in one application cycle. According to Pacific University's guide, most students keep their list between 5 and 10 schools.

At this stage it is important that the student can explain why they want to study at each school. We suggest you build the list not only by rankings but also by the program's course content and the family's budget.

Feedback on written texts, not authorship

In the United Kingdom the personal statement now consists of the answers to three questions: why the student wants the subject, how their courses prepared them and what they did outside school. The three answers together cannot be more than 4,000 characters.

UCAS wants the text to be entirely the student's own work and nothing to be copied from the internet. So a consultant should not write the text on the student's behalf. Their job is to ask the right questions, give feedback on drafts and help the student find their own voice.

Visas and documents: a checklist, not a promise of results

The university makes the acceptance decision, and the authorities of the relevant country make the visa decision. IRCC writes that no one can promise that an application will get special treatment or be approved. The University of Canberra also stresses that the outcome of enrolment and visa can never be promised for certain.

The consultant's job at this stage is to draw up the country's official document list for the student's situation and to notice what is missing in time. Telling official channels apart is part of this:

  • The University of Edinburgh reminds students that official UK government websites always end in “.gov.uk”.
  • The University of Leeds writes that the Home Office will not phone a student to ask for an extra payment or personal information.

Questions you can ask when choosing a consultant

The universities' own pages give useful tips on this. When you meet a consultant, we suggest you ask these questions:

  • Are they on the university's approved agent list? The University of Liverpool says it works only with approved agents and publishes the list on its site.
  • Are they independent? The University of Canberra writes that some consultants receive payment from universities and may therefore promote certain programs.
  • Are the fees and the scope of service in writing? Canberra suggests getting all fees and the agreement details in writing before signing.
  • Do they promise acceptance or a visa? Canberra advises being careful about firm promises of this kind.
  • Do they have a certificate? Canberra suggests checking the consultant's certificate in ICEF's Qualified Education Agent (QUA) list.

Liverpool also writes that in cases such as misleading information or pressure, a student can make a complaint and this will not affect the application.

Support for the family over a long process

The application period is long. Common App opens on 1 August, and some decisions come in the spring of the next year. For example, at Johns Hopkins, Early Decision II decisions are released on 12 February 2027. During this time, waiting, rejections and talks about a plan B can be tiring for the family.

An experienced consultant can explain what is normal, make the next step clear and build a bridge between the student and the family. We suggest you decide whether you need this support by looking at your family's time and experience.
